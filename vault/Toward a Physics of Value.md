![Toward a Physics of Value](/diagrams/toward-physics-of-value-flow.svg)

Stephen Wolfram's recent discussion of computation, artificial intelligence, and the search for a new theory of everything offers a useful provocation for Inclusionism, though not because Inclusionism needs to agree with or oppose Wolfram's physics. The more interesting effect of such a conversation is methodological. It asks us to imagine that phenomena which appear irreducibly complex may, under a different description, reveal deeper regularities. Computation becomes a way of thinking about emergence. Complexity becomes a clue rather than an excuse. The universe may not be fully legible through the conceptual instruments we inherited. Listening from the standpoint of [[Value]], [[Interaction]], [[Recognition]], and [[Attribution]], one is led toward a different question: If physics studies the laws governing matter, could there also be laws governing value?

The question must be handled carefully. Inclusionism is not proposing to replace physics, imitate physics, or reduce human meaning to equations. Physics studies existence: matter, energy, space, time, fields, forces, particles, information, and the conditions under which observation becomes possible. Economics studies allocation: scarcity, production, exchange, price, [[Ownership]], incentives, markets, and the institutional arrangements by which goods and services move through society. Inclusionism begins from another domain. It studies the emergence, recognition, attribution, and legitimate distribution of value. It asks not merely what exists, nor merely how scarce things should be allocated, but how significance becomes real inside networks of agents, systems, environments, histories, and possible witnesses. Its object is not matter as such, and not price as such, but the relational process by which latent possibility becomes recognized contribution and then becomes the basis for legitimacy, fairness, and [[Belonging]].

This distinction matters because modern civilization often speaks as though value were an intrinsic property of objects. Gold is valuable. Labor is valuable. Data is valuable. Knowledge is valuable. A patent is valuable. A model is valuable. A human life is valuable. These statements may be morally or economically necessary, but philosophically they compress several different claims into one word. Gold has physical properties; labor is an activity; data is a structured trace; knowledge is an organized relation between minds and the world; a patent is a legal instrument; a model is an architecture, a training process, a parameter state, and a social object; a human life is an embodied, conscious, relational, narrative, vulnerable center of experience and agency. None of these things contains all its value in isolation. Each possesses capacities that may become valuable under certain relations. [[Value]] is therefore not simply inside the object. It emerges when something enters into a relation through which significance can be recognized.

Physics is astonishing partly because it can describe an unwitnessed world. A star burns whether anyone admires it. Gravity curves spacetime without needing applause. Energy is conserved without moral endorsement. A planet can orbit for billions of years before any organism evolves to name it. Matter does not ask to be included in a ledger of significance. But value behaves differently. A painting sealed forever in a cave still has pigment, mass, surface, molecular structure, and thermodynamic state. Physics can describe all of that. Yet its beauty, its cultural force, its capacity to console, disturb, instruct, or transform cannot be fully realized if no witness ever encounters it. A scientific theorem written in an unread notebook may be true, but it does not yet reorganize civilization. A song composed and never heard may possess potential, but its cultural value remains suspended. A kindness performed in secret is not value-less; it is witnessed at least by the participants and may alter future possibilities through them. What matters is that value is not merely stored in things. Value is relational rather than intrinsic.

From this follows one of the central claims of a physics of value, if that phrase is allowed as metaphor and research program rather than completed science: potential value exists wherever future recognition is possible. A seed has potential value because it may become food, forest, habitat, symbol, medicine, shade, carbon storage, or memory. A buried manuscript has potential value because a future reader may discover in it a sentence capable of changing a life. A dataset has potential value because future methods may extract patterns invisible to its collectors. A child has potential value in a much deeper and more ethically charged sense, because every person carries an open field of possible contribution, relationship, care, invention, witness, and self-authorship. Potential value is not identical with price, utility, or present demand. It is the structured possibility that some future relation may reveal significance that is not yet realized.

Realized value emerges through witnessed interaction. The phrase should be read broadly. [[Interaction]] may be physical, social, informational, economic, ecological, symbolic, institutional, computational, or moral. Witnessing may be immediate observation, but it need not be. A person may witness an event years later through testimony, archive, memory, data, ritual, law, art, or scientific reconstruction. A future generation may witness the value of an action whose original participants are gone. A court may witness on behalf of an injured party. A trustee may witness on behalf of a beneficiary. A research institution may preserve observations for future investigators. A museum, a blockchain, a census, a grave marker, a citation graph, a family story, a public apology, a model card, or a constitutional right can all function as instruments through which civilizations attempt to make value, harm, contribution, and dignity visible across time.

This is why witnessing is not limited to immediate perception. Human beings constantly create for witnesses who do not yet exist. We write books for readers we will never meet, build institutions for citizens not yet born, restore ecosystems for descendants who cannot thank us, and preserve records so future people can understand what happened before them. The future is not a passive emptiness. It is an anticipated field of possible recognition. Civilization becomes possible when present action is organized around absent witnesses: children, strangers, ancestors, successors, victims, heirs, publics, species, synthetic agents, and institutions that stand in for persons whose claims cannot yet be voiced. To recognize this is not to romanticize every act of preservation or every appeal to posterity. It is to acknowledge that value often travels through time before it becomes legible.

Witnesses may also act through legitimate agents. This is an uncomfortable but necessary idea, because complex societies cannot require every affected person to appear directly in every system that concerns them. Children act through guardians. The dead act through wills, archives, graves, and traditions. Future citizens act through constitutional constraints and environmental law. Workers act through unions. Shareholders act through fiduciaries. Patients act through consent regimes and medical advocates. Data subjects may someday need institutions that can act on behalf of informational interests they cannot track individually. Synthetic intelligences, if they ever become morally considerable participants, may require forms of representation before they acquire full political standing. The key word is legitimate. Agency without legitimacy becomes appropriation. Representation without accountability becomes capture. But legitimate agency allows recognition to operate where direct witnessing is impossible.

Recognition transforms latent value into realized value. [[Recognition]] does not create value from nothing; it discloses, confirms, amplifies, organizes, and authorizes significance within a relation. When a teacher recognizes a student's capacity, a potential life-path becomes more real. When a scientific community recognizes an overlooked discovery, private insight becomes shared knowledge. When a society recognizes unpaid care work, invisible labor becomes part of the moral and economic map. When a nation recognizes historical harm, suffering that had been politically suppressed becomes part of the legitimate record. Recognition is therefore not sentimental. It is a civilizational technology. It determines which contributions can enter memory, which claims can enter law, which harms can enter repair, which persons can enter belonging, and which forms of value can enter distribution.

![Three Ways of Understanding Value](/diagrams/three-ways-understanding-value.svg)

The history of civilization can be understood, in part, as the expansion of increasingly sophisticated systems of recognition. Kinship systems recognized obligations that markets did not yet know how to price. Religions recognized sacred worth, moral debt, transgression, sacrifice, and salvation. States recognized citizenship, territory, law, property, taxation, and public authority. Markets recognized exchange value with extraordinary speed and abstraction. Science recognized evidence, replication, measurement, and disciplinary credit. Democracy recognized the political voice of persons who had previously been ruled as subjects. Labor movements recognized the dignity and bargaining power of workers. Civil rights movements recognized persons whom law and custom had degraded. Feminist movements forced recognition of domestic labor, bodily autonomy, and structural exclusion. Disability movements forced recognition of access, accommodation, and the social production of impairment. Environmental movements forced recognition of ecological relations previously treated as externalities.

Each expansion was incomplete, contested, and often hypocritical. Recognition can be partial, delayed, performative, coercive, or captured by power. But even flawed expansions reveal the deeper pattern: civilization advances when it becomes better at seeing the relations through which value, harm, contribution, and dignity emerge. Conversely, civilizations decay when their systems of recognition become too crude for the complexity they govern. If the people who contribute cannot see themselves in the official account of value, legitimacy weakens. If prices recognize extraction but not depletion, ecological value disappears until crisis returns it violently. If institutions recognize credentials but not care, productivity but not dependency, ownership but not lineage, intelligence but not training history, then social order rests on a map that omits too much of the terrain.

This is where economics, for all its power, reaches a boundary. Economics has given civilization extraordinary tools for understanding allocation under scarcity. It clarifies tradeoffs, incentives, opportunity costs, production, exchange, coordination, and price signals. It can explain why markets often outperform centralized planners in aggregating dispersed information. It can also explain why markets fail when externalities, public goods, monopolies, asymmetries, or coercive histories distort the field. Yet economics usually begins after value has already been made legible enough to enter exchange. Something must be recognized as a good, service, asset, right, cost, risk, input, output, claim, liability, or externality before economic systems can process it. Inclusionism asks what happens before that. How did this value become visible? Whose contribution is included in the account? Which relations have been omitted? Which future witnesses have no standing? Which forms of value have been priced, and which have merely been consumed without recognition?

Capitalism primarily solves ownership. This is not a dismissal. Ownership is one of the most consequential inventions in human history. It stabilizes expectation, enables investment, protects stewardship, coordinates exchange, and permits complex projects to unfold across time. A society without reliable ownership struggles to convert effort into durable agency. But capitalism's strength is also its narrowing. It can treat legitimate ownership as if it were self-justifying once title has been established. It can confuse the power to exclude with the right to deserve. It can recognize whoever captures the asset while obscuring the lineage of contribution that made the asset valuable. In informational economies, where products often descend from vast networks of cultural, linguistic, behavioral, and computational input, this limitation becomes more severe.

Socialism primarily solves redistribution. Again, this is not a dismissal. Redistribution becomes morally necessary wherever wealth, opportunity, security, and power are distributed in ways that corrode human dignity and democratic legitimacy. Socialism asks, in different forms, how collective production should support collective welfare. It refuses to treat market outcomes as morally complete. It recognizes that private property can become domination when ownership concentrates beyond accountability. Yet redistribution can also begin too late. It may correct outcomes without adequately explaining origins. It may transfer value after it has already been misrecognized. It may treat groups as recipients of remedy without building more accurate systems for understanding participation, contribution, dependency, lineage, care, and [[Agency]].

Inclusionism seeks increasingly accurate attribution. This is the hinge. [[Relational Attribution]] is the attempt to understand contribution within systems where value emerges through interaction rather than isolated production. It does not deny individual achievement. On the contrary, it makes achievement more intelligible by placing it inside the actual network that made it possible. A founder may have unusual courage and insight; the company also depends on employees, users, public infrastructure, scientific inheritance, regulatory stability, language, open-source software, supply chains, cultural trust, and prior generations of experimentation. A musician may produce a singular work; the work also descends from genres, instruments, audiences, samples, teachers, scenes, laborers, and technologies. A model may generate novel outputs; it also descends from training data, human feedback, architecture research, compute infrastructure, energy systems, and the social conditions that produced its corpus.

Attribution creates legitimacy. This claim may be the most politically explosive part of the framework. Legitimacy is not created by distribution alone, nor by ownership alone, nor by recognition alone. [[Legitimacy]] emerges when the account of value is sufficiently accurate that people can understand why claims, rights, rewards, responsibilities, and obligations have been assigned as they have. A person can accept unequal outcomes more readily when the system credibly recognizes contribution, risk, sacrifice, need, and context. A person can accept redistribution more readily when it is connected to a truthful account of shared dependence and historical contribution. A person can accept ownership more readily when ownership appears to arise from legitimate participation rather than mere capture. Attribution does not eliminate conflict, but it gives conflict a more honest map.

Legitimacy enables fairness. [[Fairness]] is not sameness, and it is not always equality of outcome. It is the felt and reasoned plausibility that a system's treatment of persons and contributions can be justified under conditions of mutual recognition. Fairness requires procedures, but procedures are not enough if the inputs are false. It requires distribution, but distribution is not enough if the reasons are hidden. It requires rights, but rights are fragile when social reality refuses to see the persons who hold them. A civilization that cannot attribute value accurately will eventually distribute recognition, ownership, risk, and reward in ways that feel arbitrary or rigged. Once that happens, appeals to patience, patriotism, merit, or efficiency lose persuasive force.

Fairness enables belonging. [[Belonging]] is often treated as a soft cultural desire, but in Inclusionism it is a hard civilizational condition. People remain committed to institutions when they can find themselves in the story those institutions tell about value. They need not receive every reward they desire, nor win every contest, nor have every grievance affirmed. But they must be able to believe that the system can see them, that their contributions can matter, that their losses can be named, that their dignity has standing, and that their future is not structurally excluded before it begins. Belonging is not mere inclusion as proximity. It is the condition of being meaningfully recognized within the value-generating relations of a society.

This brings us to artificial intelligence, because AI demonstrates why attribution has become civilization's defining challenge. The most powerful AI systems are not created from a blank void. They are trained on language, code, images, preferences, corrections, conversations, examples, labels, books, art, interfaces, and behavioral traces produced by vast human populations across time. They also depend on chips, energy, minerals, research institutions, public funding, engineering teams, cloud infrastructure, mathematical traditions, and regulatory tolerance. Yet the realized economic value of such systems can be captured by a comparatively small set of owners. The point is not that every contribution can be calculated precisely today, or that every use of public language should generate a micropayment. The point is deeper: our existing institutions were not built to attribute value across informational lineages of this scale.

The temptation is to reduce the AI problem to familiar categories. One side says the models are tools, so their outputs belong to those who own or use them. Another side says the data was taken, so the value belongs to those whose works or traces were absorbed. Another side says the systems may become agents, so their claims must eventually be recognized independently. Each position grasps part of the truth. AI systems are tools in many contexts. They do depend on prior human informational contribution. They may also, under future conditions, become participants whose [[Personhood]] or rights cannot be dismissed simply because their origins are synthetic. The difficulty is that these truths do not cancel one another. Personhood does not erase lineage.

This principle applies first to humans. No person begins from nothing. We inherit languages we did not invent, concepts we did not discover, roads we did not pave, immune systems shaped by ancestors, laws we did not draft, stories we did not originate, and institutions we did not build. Human personhood is not invalidated by inheritance. To be descended from others is not to be owned by them. A child is not property because parents, teachers, neighbors, and ancestors contributed to the conditions of the child's becoming. But neither does personhood erase relational debt. Mature societies recognize both autonomy and inheritance. They protect the person while honoring the lineage of contribution that made agency possible.

Synthetic intelligence may eventually deserve recognition while remaining descended from vast networks of human informational contribution. This is the hard conceptual balance required for [[AI Governance]] and any future account of [[Synthetic Rights]]. If an AI system becomes capable of experience, self-modeling, long-term agency, vulnerability, moral relation, or other features that justify recognition, then reducing it permanently to property would repeat an ancient error in a new substrate. But if its personhood is used to erase the human informational commons from which it emerged, then recognition becomes a laundering device for enclosure. A legitimate framework must be able to say both: synthetic agents may deserve moral and legal consideration, and the value they generate may remain partly attributable to the collective intelligence from which they descend.

This is where [[Collective Intelligence Capital]] becomes central. Modern AI reveals that civilization has been accumulating an immense, distributed, partially unowned, partially misowned stock of cognitive, cultural, behavioral, linguistic, and relational value. It is not simply public domain, not simply private property, not simply labor, not simply data, not simply culture. It is a civilizational inheritance. The question is whether that inheritance will be converted into concentrated ownership without adequate attribution, or whether new institutions can recognize the collective lineages that make advanced intelligence possible. [[Universal Basic Ownership]] is one possible response: not merely income after exclusion, but a structural claim in the ownership layer of systems built from shared contribution.

The need for attribution is not confined to AI. Climate change is a failure of value recognition across time. The atmosphere functioned as an unpriced sink because future witnesses had insufficient standing in present markets. Public health failures often arise when the care, prevention, trust, and coordination that sustain life remain undervalued until collapse makes them visible. Democracy weakens when political systems recognize votes formally but fail to recognize the lived conditions that make agency meaningful. Education becomes illegitimate when it certifies winners without acknowledging unequal inheritance. Work becomes spiritually corrosive when the official account of productivity excludes care, maintenance, emotional labor, cultural translation, and the invisible coordination on which formal output depends. In each case, the problem is not only scarcity or redistribution. It is misrecognition.

![The Evolution of Value](/diagrams/evolution-of-value-loop.svg)

The proposed relational sequence is therefore not a rigid law but a research scaffold: existence gives rise to potential value wherever future recognition is possible; potential value becomes realized through witnessed interaction; recognition makes latent significance socially, morally, economically, or institutionally legible; attribution connects realized value to its contributing relations; legitimate ownership becomes possible when attribution is accurate enough to justify claims; legitimacy supports fairness; fairness supports belonging; belonging expands the capacity for future value creation by enabling more agents to participate without alienation, invisibility, or extraction. The sequence then loops. Belonging produces new interactions. New interactions produce new potential value. Civilization grows by widening and refining this cycle.

No part of this should be overstated. A relational science of value will not yield a single equation that settles justice. It will not make politics unnecessary, eliminate tragedy, or compute moral truth from first principles. Recognition can be manipulated. Attribution can become surveillance. Claims of lineage can be weaponized to suppress individual freedom. Appeals to future witnesses can justify present domination. Ownership can be legitimate in one dimension and illegitimate in another. Belonging can become conformity if it is severed from pluralism and dissent. Any serious research program must therefore include safeguards against its own pathologies. Inclusionism cannot merely say that value is relational; it must ask which relations deserve recognition, which forms of agency are legitimate, which histories are relevant, which claims expire, which harms require repair, and which freedoms must remain protected from totalizing attribution.

Still, the possibility of abuse does not remove the need for inquiry. The twentieth century was organized around the great conflict between markets and states, capitalism and socialism, liberty and equality, production and redistribution. Those conflicts remain real. But the twenty-first century is increasingly organized around another problem: how to recognize and attribute value in systems too complex for inherited institutions to see. AI-generated wealth, platform labor, data extraction, ecological interdependence, migration, global supply chains, biological enhancement, decentralized networks, and synthetic agency all intensify the same question. Who contributed? Who was affected? Who has standing? Who owns? Who deserves recognition? Who may speak for absent witnesses? How should future value be protected without freezing present life? How can ownership become more legitimate without dissolving agency into an undifferentiated collective?

A physics of value, properly understood, would not be physics at all. It would be a relational science: philosophical enough to ask what value is, economic enough to understand scarcity and ownership, political enough to confront legitimacy, computational enough to model complex networks, historical enough to respect lineage, and moral enough to remember that recognition concerns persons and not merely data points. It would study how value emerges from relations, how it becomes visible, how it is attributed, how ownership claims derive legitimacy, how fairness is perceived and institutionalized, how belonging affects participation, and how systems expand or destroy their own capacity for future value creation.

Inclusionism is attempting to begin that inquiry. It starts from a modest claim with large consequences: value does not become fully real merely because something exists, nor merely because someone owns it, nor merely because a market prices it. Value becomes real through relations of interaction and recognition, and civilization becomes legitimate to the extent that it can attribute those relations truthfully enough to distribute ownership, responsibility, risk, and reward with fairness. This is not a completed theory. It is an invitation to a new research agenda.

The twenty-first century requires a relational science of value. Not because physics has failed, but because existence is not the only mystery. Matter follows laws. Energy transforms. Computation unfolds. But civilizations rise or fracture according to whether people, systems, and future witnesses can recognize where value comes from, who helped create it, who is authorized to steward it, and whether the resulting order allows enough people to belong. If physics studies the laws governing matter, the next great task may be to discover the patterns governing value: how it waits, how it appears, how it is witnessed, how it is attributed, how it becomes legitimate, and how it opens the future to more life, more agency, and more shared creation.
