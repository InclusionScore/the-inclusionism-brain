# Recognition

## Definition

The institutional and relational act of seeing that an agent exists, participates, contributes, is affected, or belongs within a system.

---

## Inclusionist Perspective

Recognition transforms [[Witnessing]] into social legitimacy.

Recognition is not merely symbolic affirmation. It is the discipline through which systems learn who is present, how people participate, what relationships shape them, which contributions they make, which risks they carry, and how value moves through the system.

---

## Core Principle

Participation becomes meaningful when it is recognized.

Recognition precedes [[Relational Attribution]] because institutions must first understand who participated before they can attribute value, risk, responsibility, or benefit.

---

## Inclusionist Perspective

Many forms of exclusion emerge not from hostility but from failures of recognition.

Unrecognized participation often produces unrecognized value.

Institutions cannot govern people they refuse to see.

Identity provides context for recognition, but it does not determine moral worth. Seeing identity is not the same as judging identity. [[Identification]] is not discrimination when it is used to understand participation, contribution, consequence, and agency rather than to deny dignity or fix status.

## Recognition Requires Seeing People

Recognition is sometimes confused with acknowledging diversity, naming demographic categories, or performing symbolic inclusion. Inclusionism uses the term more rigorously. Recognition is the institutional discipline of accurately identifying participants, understanding relationships, observing contributions, witnessing interactions, and seeing how value and risk are distributed.

To recognize a person is not merely to notice that they belong to a category. It is to understand how they are situated inside a living system. A person participates through labor, care, data, memory, creativity, coordination, risk, trust, consumption, dissent, dependency, and repair. These forms of participation are often invisible to institutions that only see formal roles, titles, transactions, or credentials.

Recognition therefore requires identification. Institutions must know who is present, who is affected, who contributes, who depends on whom, and who bears consequences. Identification is not discrimination. Discrimination uses identity to exclude, rank, diminish, or predetermine worth. Recognition uses identity as relational information: context that helps institutions understand participation, contribution, consequence, and [[Agency]].

Seeing is not judging. A just institution does not pretend that identity, lineage, location, class, race, gender, disability, language, data traces, or institutional position are irrelevant. It also does not reduce a person to any of those facts. Identity provides context; it does not determine moral worth.

This is why recognition is prior to [[Relational Attribution]]. A system cannot attribute value accurately if it does not first see who participated in creating it. It cannot distribute opportunity fairly if it does not understand who was excluded from participation. It cannot assign responsibility legitimately if it cannot distinguish contribution from coercion, consent from dependency, or formal membership from actual agency.

InclusionScore's methodology begins from this same philosophical premise. An organizational assessment starts with a Service Catalog because an institution must first learn what it actually does, who it serves, who participates in each service, and where value and risk travel. A Service Catalog is not merely an operational inventory. It is an act of institutional self-recognition.

Feedback Mechanisms extend that recognition outward. They allow participants, workers, communities, users, and affected groups to make hidden experience visible to the institution. Feedback is not only a measurement tool. It is a practice of institutional sight: a way for organizations to recognize themselves through the people they affect.

Together, Service Catalogs and Feedback Mechanisms teach organizations how to see. They reveal whether formal structures match lived reality, whether value is being recognized where it actually emerges, whether risks are accumulating out of sight, and whether participants possess enough agency to shape the systems they help sustain.

Recognition is therefore not a soft virtue added after governance. It is a precondition of governance. To govern for the people, an institution must first see the people.
