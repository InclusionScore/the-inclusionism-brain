# Religious Freedom

## Definition
The freedom of individuals and communities to develop, practice, interpret, question, change, leave, or decline systems of spiritual meaning without coercion.

---

## Inclusionist Perspective
Plural meaning systems increase civilizational diversity and adaptive resilience. The right to participate in religious life and the right not to participate are expressions of the same commitment to [[Agency]], [[Individual Liberty]], and [[Pluralism]].

**No belief deserves immunity from criticism, and no person should lose their agency because of what they believe. The purpose of secularism is not to abolish religion but to prevent any religion from monopolizing the conditions of human freedom.**

Freedom of conscience protects believers, nonbelievers, doubters, converts, and those who reinterpret their traditions. It includes the freedom to criticize religious and secular claims, to advocate for or against them, and to change one's mind. Criticism of an idea is not, by itself, coercion of a person who holds it. Nor does a sincere conviction authorize its holder to govern another person's conscience.

Secular institutions are subject to the same standard. A government or institution that compels unbelief merely substitutes secular domination for religious domination. The Inclusionist concern is not whether a person chooses religious observance or a secular life, but whether the choice is meaningfully theirs and whether its exercise unjustifiably constrains the agency of others.

Voluntary observance, including practices others find restrictive, can express self-determination. Imposed observance, forced abandonment of belief, discriminatory exclusion, and punishment for dissent require separate scrutiny of the power exercised and the consequences imposed.

## Criticism, Power, and Context
No religion, philosophy, or secular doctrine has a right to be shielded from reasoned disagreement, satire, or challenge. But the legitimacy of a particular interaction is not established simply because its target is a belief rather than a person. [[Contextual Legitimacy and Relational Interpretation]] asks whose agency is exercised, who holds relevant power, and who bears the consequences. [[Dignity, Satire, and Consequential Ridicule]] distinguishes challenging a doctrine or institution from ridicule that foreseeably burdens people unrelated to the criticized conduct.

The same standard applies to dominant and minority traditions. Defending the right to criticize a belief does not justify harassment, threats, or coercive restrictions on those who hold it. Protecting believers against domination does not entitle their beliefs or institutions to immunity from accountability.

## Questions
- Can secular systems preserve spiritual plurality without becoming a competing orthodoxy?
- How should civilization negotiate conflicting belief systems when their practices affect others' agency?
- When does criticism of a belief become an exercise of domination over believers?
- Who has the authority to define the boundaries of conscience, and how may that authority be challenged?
